# From Reservoir Dogs to Hollywood: Does Violence Define Tarantino's Work?
![tarantino-1024x576](https://hackmd.io/_uploads/rkI12bgeeg.jpg)

Quentin Tarantino is an American filmmaker, actor, and author who, over the past 30 years, has left his mark within many spaces, including cinema and pop culture. Although his cutting-edge dialogue and extravagant storytelling may help his name go down as one of the most exciting directors of the early 21st century, what solidifies it is his impenitent use of one thing…violence. His shocking yet stylistic use of bloodshed is inevitable throughout his filmography, including both exclusively directed or collaboration projects.

From the start, Tarantino has been definitive that he would only release 10 movies. With the recent news that his 10th and final film has been scrapped and the filmmaker is returning to the drawing board, it seemed only relevant to delve into his previous filmography and the range of fatalities within his deadly masterpieces. 

Whether it is the vengeful bride effortlessly slicing through dozens in Kill Bill or the stylish brutality of Pulp Fiction, each movie can rack up its fair share of bodies. Within this blog, Tarantino's filmography is explored through the lens of violence and how Tarantino's attitude towards death varies from movie to movie.
 
 
## Importance of Violence within Viewer Perception

### Kill Count Explained:
From the graph, it is clear that Inglorious Basterds (2009) has a significant death toll throughout its run time, with over 300 deaths, further solidifying it as Tarantino's highest death toll throughout his whole filmography. Kill Bill: Vol 1 (2003) has just under a third of the kills in Inglourious Basterds. Although both of these tolls reflect their storylines, being that vengeance and violence, amongst other factors, are the driving forces of these narratives, Tarantino considers the context of these movies. Whilst Inglourious Basterds' death toll aligns with the mass execution and alternate history narrative, it runs a lower yet respectable kill count in Kill Bill: Vol 1, which is reflected in the large-scale fight scenes. On the other hand, Tarantino doesn't rely on death to make an infatuating story, given that Once Upon a Time... in Hollywood (2019) and Jackie Brown (1997) have only four kills throughout the film. Taking up the middle ground, The Hateful Eight (2015) and Django Unchained (2012) each show a moderate level of kills (70 and 55, respectively). Although deaths are executed in a very effective way within both of these films, it could be said that a relatively lower death toll compared to movies such as Inglourious Basterds is again due to Tarantino respecting the context of his films. This is especially evident within his 'period pieces,'’ such as in Django Unchained, where violence is merely a response to the brutal system of slavery that runs in this world. Therefore, the need for an astronomically high kill count is unnecessary. On the other hand, in Inglourious Basterds,  the 'Inglourious Basterds's' whole role was to inflict violence and terror upon Nazi-occupied France, hence further explaining the higher death toll.

### Are IMDb ratings related to kill count?

Throughout all of Tarantino's filmography, most films maintain a rating between 7.5 and 8.9. Ultimately, this suggests that the level of violence has a limited effect on the audience's reception and shows that audiences value Tarantino's storytelling and character development craftmanship. Although Death Proof has both low kills and the lowest rating, there is not enough data to suggest a correlation between kill counts and high ratings, given that Pulp Fiction holds a low kill count whilst enjoying the highest rating of 8.9.

Overall, this graph solidifies Tarantino's success as a storyteller. It shows that despite his synonymity with violence, it is merely a tool for storytelling and not a cheap method for audience shock factor. His acknowledgment of the context and history behind his stories are reflected in these death tolls and shows appropriate levels of respect for his characters. 

## Does Tarantino treat Male and Female characters differently?
![Tarantino Photo #2](https://hackmd.io/_uploads/BkYAK2xgxe.jpg)


### Male vs Female: The Killers

Looking at these pie charts, it is clear that Tarantino's filmography contains a strong male dominance in the portrayal of violence. There is a constant pattern within his films that violence is an exclusively male trait, with six out of nine of his films having 100% of all kills carried out by men. Although films like Kill Bill Vol. 1 & 2 alter this narrative, with 87.2% of kills being carried out by women, the motives behind these women being killers are different. In Kill Bill, Vol. 1 & 2, violence and the ability to kill are used as means of empowerment, whereas in other male-dominated films, killing doesn't need justification. Tarantino appears to only give violent agency to women in specific contexts where he can justify their actions as revenge. On the other hand, movies such as Pulp Fiction, Jackie Brown, Reservoir Dogs, Django Unchained, and Once Upon a Time in Hollywood show killing as a domain only his male characters can engage within. 

![% of kills done per gender](https://hackmd.io/_uploads/Skts_Tgelx.png)


### Male vs Female: The Kills

Similarly to Tarantino's perspectives of gender when dealing with his killers, the gender distribution of fatalities is skewed in favor of his male characters. The stacked bar chart shows that the death of Tarantino's male characters in nearly every movie is more prominent. This is especially evident in Inglourious Basters (260 male deaths), Django Unchained, and The Hateful Eight, showing a high mortality rate of men. In this case, however, his bias towards male deaths is an example of him taking into account the context of his films. All three movies are set within male-dominated environments, such as war and criminal underworlds. Equally, these films' storylines are focused around a group of men and their conflicts, hence justifying the notable inequality between male and female lives disposed of, as both the pie charts and bar charts show that although men are primarily victims, they are also the more prominent executors. Kill Bill Vol 1 & 2 again subverts this dynamic to some extent as just over 10 deaths are male, and just under 10 deaths are female. Given that the protagonist of Kill Bill Vol 1 & 2 is a woman, Tarantino again emphasizes male fatalities to empower his female characters. 

![gender distribution of deaths](https://hackmd.io/_uploads/Bk5ZPcxxgx.png)


Despite this, it should be noted that Tarantino doesn't need to rely on the gender of those who die. Within Kill Bill Vol 1 & 2, Inglourious Basterds, The Hateful Eight, and Django Unchained, there are relatively higher quantities of death of the "Unknown" gender. Although within these films, it could be due to large-scale massacres where gender is hard to find, it also demonstrates Tarantino doesn't need to use the gender of characters to evoke emotion from his audience. Equally, his disregard for needing to define gender could show that these characters who die are just considered as a body; therefore, to some extent, it could show his lack of respect for human life. However, given that Tarantino is known for his stylized and large-scale violence, especially within those sets of films, it can be said that extensive deaths regardless of gender are inevitably going to be emotive.

Overall, the data above demonstrates that Tarantino primarily deems violence a male-dominated trait, specifically male-on-male violence. Although gender roles are occasionally subverted, such as in Kill Bill Vol 1 & 2 and Death Proof, Tarantino feels the need to justify the killers and those who they kill, whilst this emphasis is not placed nearly as intensely within the rest of his filmography. Furthermore, it could be said that when he does subvert the narrative, it stands out more and interrupts Tarantino's typical pattern. This could contribute to why Kill Bill is one of Tarantino's most successful films.

## Does Tarantino have a weapon of choice?

![Photo #3](https://hackmd.io/_uploads/SyOiqnexel.jpg)


Tarantino's decisions when deciding how his characters die further prove his mastery. Across his whole filmography, the methods of violence used are tailored to compliment his narrative and the context behind his storytelling, and not simply for the shock factor. His most violent films shown in the graph above are Inglourious Basterds, Kill Bill Vol. 1, The Hateful Eight, Django Unchained, and Pulp Fiction. , although violence is central to the plot, Tarantino acknowledges the circumstances of these plots and hence differentiates the ways people die to aid his story. 




![Kill types per body count](https://hackmd.io/_uploads/Byvpd3xgel.png)


In Inglorious Basterds, for example, 273 of the deaths shown, 256 of these are executed via shootings. This use of gunfire takes into consideration that this film is set during World War II, hence showing Tarantino's respect for his context when choosing his methods of violence; similarly, in Django Unchained whereby, roughly 88% of kills are fire-arm related, paying homage to the typical Western genre with shootouts, simultaneously framing the violence as a rebellion due to the harm inflicted due to slavery. Tarantino deviates from gun violence to bladed weapons in Kill Bill. Over 50 of the 76 deaths in Kill Bill Vol 1 are executed through swords, which again nods to the martial arts cinema that Tarantino was inspired by.

Furthermore, Pulp Fiction shows that Tarantino tactfully uses his methods of violence to drive the story rather than relying on it. Although Pulp fiction has a relatively lower death toll of 26, a range of methods, including gunshot, overdose, strangulation, and accidents, are used. This shows that Tarantino doesn't need binary types of violence to make an impact, and he uses what he deems correct for the characters.

On the other hand, the films with the lower death toll further prove Tarantino's tasteful view on violence. Although movies like Reservoir Dogs are known for their violent and shocking scenes, only 12 deaths occur. Most deaths within this film are unknown, showing that, in some cases, bloodshed isn't required, and ambiguity can add to tension rather than diminish it. Similarly, in Once Upon a Time... in Hollywood, tension is built up as the audience already knows about the Manson murders; therefore, a high death toll isn't required, and the seven kills being made by physical combat and gunfire are used to cut this tension effectively. 

Across these films, genre, and context define the kill types Tarantino uses. The data shows that although violence is inevitable throughout his filmography, the quantity of violence and how it is portrayed are used as stylistic tools and genre commentary rather than to provoke distasteful and unnecessary reactions from viewers.


## How does Tarantino treat his killers?

The heat map above shows the relationship between the number of people killed by certain characters and their subsequent fate by the movie's end. Despite Tarantino's movies being synonymous with extreme violence, the treatment of his leading killers by the finale is not uniform. This shows that the fate of those who show extreme violence is deliberate and in line with the themes and context behind the films. 

![Character Kills vs Fate](https://hackmd.io/_uploads/ry7ED3xxgg.png)



Characters such as Beatrix Kiddo (Kill Bill) and Django Freeman (Django Unchained) are among the top killers, with 81 and 41 kills, respectively. Although it could be argued that those responsible for so much death should be met with the same fate, Tarantino lets both characters live. Their decisions to unleash violence are deeply rooted in their moral framework, such that Beatrix wants vengeance for the betrayal she has undergone and the loss of motherhood she wants, whilst Django fights for freedom and those he loves. The decision to grant these people lives shows Tarantino accepts justified violence within his films. Conversely, Inglourious Basterds sees the death of almost all the Nazi-hinting killers, such as Donowitz, Utivich, and Zimmerman, who have high kill counts (over 250). Although it could be argued that their kills are justified as their victims are frequently Nazis, given the context of Inglourious Basterds, during wartime it seems rare that all violence no matter what the aim, is ultimately self-destructive. 

Ultimately, Tarantino seems to reward those who have engaged in violence with life if their intentions were personal and give the character redemption. This further shows that although Tarantino's choices may deviate from his overall taste for colorful violence, there is a moral code that he intends to follow.
